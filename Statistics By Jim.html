5 Methods for Assessing Causality in Statistics

By Jim Frost Leave a Comment

You have probably heard the familiar warning that correlation does not imply causation. Two variables can have a strong relationship without changes in one variable actually producing changes in the other. Assessing causality is crucial when you want to change an outcome.

Suppose a regression analysis finds that an independent variable has a strong relationship with the dependent variable. Even a large coefficient and a small p-value do not automatically establish that changing the independent variable will cause the dependent variable to change. Confounding variables, reverse causation, trends over time, and other factors can produce associations that look causal when they are not.

That distinction matters whenever you want to move beyond asking, “Are these variables related?” and start asking, “What would happen if we changed this variable?”

In this post, you will learn five methods for assessing causality. I present them in an approximate progression from weaker to stronger evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship. Each successive method addresses limitations of the methods that come before it, although every approach requires assumptions and works best in certain situations.

Moving from Correlation to Cause and Effect

Causal inference requires more than finding statistically significant relationships.

Regression analysis can identify independent variables associated with a dependent variable while controlling statistically for other measured variables. However, including variables in a regression model does not automatically turn an observational relationship into a causal one. Unmeasured confounding, reverse causation, selection effects, and other problems can remain.

The more useful causal question is not merely:

“Does X relate to Y?”

Instead, ask:

“If I intervened and changed X, how would Y respond?”

Correlation can tell you that variables move together, but causal inference asks a more demanding question: Would changing one variable actually change another?

That change in perspective is fundamental to causal inference.

Learn more about Correlation vs. Causation.

1. Granger Causality: Does One Variable Help Predict Another Over Time?

Granger causality applies to time series data, where you repeatedly measure variables over time.

Despite its name, Granger causality does not establish causation in the usual sense. Instead, it evaluates whether past values of one variable improve predictions of another variable beyond what you can predict from the dependent variable’s own past values.

Suppose you want to predict monthly electricity consumption. You suspect that past outdoor temperatures contain useful information about future electricity use.

The basic procedure compares two models. The first predicts future electricity consumption using previous values of electricity consumption. The second includes both previous electricity consumption and previous temperature measurements.

If adding past temperatures significantly improves the prediction, temperature is said to Granger-cause electricity consumption.

That result tells you something useful: one time series contains predictive information about another, and the timing is consistent with a possible causal relationship.

However, that is a long way from proving cause and effect.

For example, seasonal changes might influence both temperature and electricity consumption. If you fail to model seasonality adequately, temperature might appear to provide causal information when both variables are responding to the same underlying seasonal process.

A third variable can create similar problems. Granger causality establishes temporal predictive information, not the absence of confounding variables. Learn more about Confounding Variables because understanding them is crucial for assessing causality!

Consequently, I consider this the weakest of the five methods for establishing causality. It can identify interesting relationships and possible causal directions, but additional evidence is necessary before making strong causal claims.

2. Difference-in-Differences Causality: Compare Changes Rather Than Levels

Difference-in-differences (DiD) can provide stronger evidence for causality when you have observational data involving a treatment or intervention that affects one group but not another.

Rather than simply comparing a treatment group with a control group after an intervention, DiD compares how both groups change over time.

Suppose one manufacturing facility introduces a new safety training program while a similar facility continues its existing procedures. You want to determine whether the new program reduces workplace injuries.

Simply comparing injury rates at the two facilities after the program begins would be problematic. The facilities might have differed before the intervention.

You also should not simply compare injury rates at the treatment facility before and after the training program. Injury rates might have changed over time for reasons unrelated to the training.

Difference-in-differences uses both comparisons.

Suppose the injury rate at the treatment facility decreases by 20%, while the control facility experiences a 5% decline over the same period. The difference-in-differences estimate attributes the additional 15-percentage-point decline to the intervention, assuming the method’s requirements hold.

The control group helps estimate what would have happened to the treatment group without the intervention. In causal terminology, it helps estimate the counterfactual.

This approach addresses an important problem that Granger causality does not: DiD explicitly incorporates a comparison control group to account for background changes occurring over time. It also removes the effects of group characteristics that remain constant over the study period.

DiD Challenges

However, confounding variables remain a major concern.

The critical assumption is usually called the parallel trends assumption. Without the intervention, the treatment and control groups should have followed similar trends over time.

Suppose that, at the same time the treatment facility introduces its safety program, it also replaces old equipment while the control facility does not. The equipment change is a confounding variable because it can affect the injury rate independently of the training program.

Similarly, changes in staffing, workloads, management practices, or other conditions that affect the two groups differently can bias the estimated treatment effect.

Consequently, DiD handles some confounding problems well, particularly stable differences between groups and trends that affect both groups similarly. But it does not automatically eliminate confounding.

Checking pre-intervention trends and considering other changes that occurred around the intervention are essential parts of a credible DiD analysis.

3. Instrumental Variables: Finding an Outside Source of Variation to Assess Causality

Instrumental variables (IV) provide another approach for assessing causality when you cannot randomly assign the treatment and are concerned about confounding variables.

The method uses a third variable, called an instrument, to create variation in the treatment.

A valid instrumental variable must satisfy several important conditions. It must affect the treatment, but it should affect the dependent variable only through that treatment. It also must not share confounding causes with the outcome in a way that violates the design.

Consider a study evaluating whether participation in an optional job-training program improves later employment outcomes.

People who voluntarily attend the program might differ systematically from those who do not. For example, participants might be more motivated, have more flexible schedules, or already have stronger employment prospects.

A simple regression comparing participants and nonparticipants therefore risks confounding.

Now suppose eligible applicants are randomly offered free transportation to the training center. Receiving the transportation offer substantially increases the probability that someone attends the training program.

The transportation offer can potentially serve as an instrumental variable. It changes participation, but if the offer has no direct effect on later employment other than through attendance at the training program, it provides an external source of variation in participation.

This is an important improvement over methods that merely adjust for measured confounding variables. A valid instrument can help address unmeasured confounding as well.

Instrumental Variable Challenges

However, the phrase valid instrument is doing a tremendous amount of work.

You must be able to defend the assumption that the instrument influences the dependent variable only through the treatment. In this example, if receiving free transportation also makes it easier for participants to attend job interviews later, the exclusion assumption could fail.

Good instruments are often difficult to find, and some of their most important assumptions cannot be tested directly from the data.

When a convincing instrument exists, IV methods can provide strong causal evidence. When the instrument is questionable, the causal conclusion becomes questionable as well.

4. Regression Discontinuity Design: Use a Treatment Cutoff to Assess Causality

Regression discontinuity design (RDD) can produce particularly strong causal evidence when assignment to a treatment depends on whether a numerical variable crosses a known threshold.

The method focuses on observations immediately above and below that cutoff.

Suppose a university automatically awards an academic support program to incoming students whose placement-test scores fall below 70. You want to know whether participation in that program improves first-year academic performance.

Comparing all students who receive the program with all students who do not would be inappropriate. Students with much lower placement scores probably differ substantially from students with much higher scores.

Instead, regression discontinuity focuses on students very close to the threshold.

A student who scores 69 receives the additional support, while a student who scores 71 does not. Those two students are likely to be much more similar than students at opposite ends of the score distribution.

If first-year performance shows a distinct jump at the score of 70, that discontinuity provides evidence that the intervention caused the difference.

The graph indicates that both groups have an upward trend where higher placement scores correspond to higher first-year performance. However, the difference at the discontinuity suggests that the students receiving the support perform better than very similar students that do not receive it. That difference is the estimated causal effect.

RDD addresses confounding in a way that the earlier observational methods generally cannot. Near the threshold, treatment and control observations can be highly comparable even without random assignment. A smooth relationship between the assignment variable and other characteristics should not suddenly produce a jump precisely at the treatment cutoff.

Regression Discontinuity Challenges

However, regression discontinuity has important limitations.

First, its causal conclusions usually apply most directly to observations near the cutoff. The treatment effect for students scoring around 70 might differ from the effect for students with much higher or lower placement scores.

Second, investigators need to verify that subjects cannot manipulate the assignment variable around the threshold. If students, administrators, or other decision makers can deliberately move observations from one side of the cutoff to the other, the groups might no longer be comparable.

Other policies or changes that occur at exactly the same threshold can also confound the estimated effect.

When those requirements hold, RDD can approximate some of the advantages of a randomized experiment and often provides highly credible causal evidence from observational data.

5. Randomized Controlled Trials and Causality: Use Randomization to Reduce Confounding

When they are feasible and properly conducted, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) generally provide the strongest design for establishing causality.

The key advantage is randomization.

Researchers randomly assign subjects to a treatment group and a control group rather than allowing treatment status to depend on subject characteristics or individual choices. Learn more about Randomized Controlled Trials and Random Assignment.

Suppose a school wants to determine whether a new study-skills program improves exam performance. Researchers randomly assign participating students either to receive the new program or to continue with the existing approach.

Because assignment is random, characteristics such as prior achievement, motivation, study habits, and many other potential confounding variables should, on average, be distributed similarly between the treatment and control groups.

Researchers can then compare subsequent exam performance between the groups.

This is what makes randomization so powerful. Difference-in-differences attempts to account for certain background differences and trends. Instrumental variables attempt to isolate variation in treatment that is independent of confounding. Regression discontinuity tries to create highly comparable groups near a cutoff.

An RCT tackles the confounding problem more directly by using random assignment from the start to equalize the groups at the beginning of the study. This preliminary equalization helps you attribute differences at the study’s end to the treatment itself. The treatment is likely to be the only systematic difference between the groups.

Randomization does not guarantee that every characteristic will be perfectly balanced in a particular sample, especially with small samples. However, it prevents systematic treatment assignment based on confounders and provides the statistical foundation for causal inference.

A/B testing is one form of randomized controlled trial. For example, a website might randomly assign visitors to two page designs and compare conversion rates. However, RCTs encompass a much broader range of experiments in many fields.

Randomized Controlled Trials Challenges

RCTs also have limitations.

Sometimes randomization is unethical, impractical, prohibitively expensive, or simply impossible. Researchers cannot randomly assign people to many potentially harmful exposures merely to determine their effects.

Even when an RCT is possible, problems such as treatment noncompliance, subjects dropping out of the study, spillover between groups, or poor measurement can complicate the analysis.

Nevertheless, when researchers can randomly assign the treatment, maintain the experimental design, and measure the outcome appropriately, RCTs generally provide the strongest protection against confounding among the methods discussed here.

Learn more about Experimental Designs and how they impact the conclusions you can draw.

Closing Thoughts on Assessing Causality

The five methods in this article approach that question with increasing protection against alternative explanations.

Granger causality can identify predictive relationships over time, but confounding can easily produce misleading conclusions. Difference-in-differences provides a stronger framework by comparing changes in treatment and control groups, although differential trends and time-varying confounders remain important concerns. Instrumental variables can address even unmeasured confounding when a valid instrument exists. Regression discontinuity can provide strong quasi-experimental evidence near a treatment threshold.

And when random assignment is possible, randomized controlled trials usually provide the strongest protection against confounding.

Understanding these differences helps you do more than choose a statistical procedure. It helps you evaluate how much causal confidence the research design actually supports. Learn more about Experimental Designs.

However, no statistical method creates causality automatically. Each method requires assumptions, and the credibility of the causal conclusion depends on how well the research design satisfies them.

The five methods above use statistical methods and research designs to help assess cause-and-effect relationships. Hill’s criteria for causality provide a different approach by considering several characteristics of the available evidence when evaluating whether an association is likely to be causal. The criteria do not prove causation, but they provide a useful framework for weighing causal evidence.

To learn more, read my post about Hill’s Criteria for Causality.

You do not need specialized software to understand the reasoning behind these methods, and many standard statistical packages can perform at least some of these analyses.

However, if you use Python, Microsoft’s DoWhy library provides tools specifically for causal inference. It encourages you to specify the assumed causal relationships explicitly, estimate causal effects using various methods, and examine whether your conclusions remain plausible under alternative assumptions.

One benefit of this type of framework is that it forces you to make your causal assumptions visible rather than leaving them implicit in a statistical model.

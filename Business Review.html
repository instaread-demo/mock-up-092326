Romania’s growth model is losing speed as productivity gains weaken – ING analysis

Romania’s potential economic growth has fallen to around 2% from 3.5-3.75% before the pandemic, with weaker productivity gains becoming a larger constraint on the economy despite still-strong investment, according to an analysis by ING Chief Economist for Romania Valentin Tătaru.

The author argues that Romania’s convergence with Western Europe is continuing, but at a slower pace. GDP per capita in purchasing power standards rose from around 44% of the EU average when Romania joined the bloc in 2007 to 78% in 2025, supported by productivity gains, foreign investment, labour reallocation and EU integration. Many of those easier sources of catch-up growth have now weakened.

ING estimates that potential GDP growth could recover to around 2.4-2.7% in 2027-2029 as the current cyclical downturn fades and the investment cycle continues. Reaching rates sustainably above 3%, however, would require stronger labour utilisation and a significantly better productivity return from investment.

The bank’s analysis comes as Romania moves through a period of weak actual growth. The economy expanded by only 0.7% in 2025 and is expected to contract in 2026, with fiscal consolidation, high inflation, weak consumption and softer external demand weighing on activity.

According to ING, the main problem is not a lack of capital formation. Gross fixed investment has recently stood at around one-quarter of GDP, while public investment has remained exceptionally high.

Roads, railways, energy infrastructure and digital networks can raise both the capital stock and productivity, but their longer-term impact will depend on whether they trigger additional private-sector investment and allow firms to operate more efficiently.

The transition after the Recovery and Resilience Facility ended in August 2026 will therefore be important. Cohesion funding will continue, but EU inflows are expected to moderate from recent peaks. ING sees the handover from public to private investment as a central test for Romania’s medium-term growth model.

If new infrastructure generates further investment in factories, logistics, energy and digital projects, the contribution from capital could remain strong and productivity could improve. If private investment fails to follow, Romania risks completing a major investment cycle without a comparable increase in its permanent growth rate, the analysis warns.

Productivity as the main weakness

ING identifies productivity as the area where most of Romania’s former growth momentum has been lost. European Commission data cited in the analysis shows real labour productivity per hour growing by around 4.5% annually between 2015 and 2019, compared with about 2% in 2020-2025.

The gap is also visible in innovation indicators. Romania spent around 0.5% of GDP on research and development in 2025, compared with 2.2% across the EU, while only 8.8% of firms introduced a product or process innovation in 2020-2022. Tertiary education attainment among 25-34-year-olds stood at 23.2%, around half the EU average.

ING argues that Romania already has highly productive companies and sectors, but has struggled to spread technology, management practices and skills more broadly across the economy.

AI and automation could help address part of that gap. The analysis notes that labour-saving technologies are particularly relevant for an ageing economy, while digital tools could help diffuse more efficient business practices beyond a relatively narrow group of high-productivity firms.

Citing World Bank estimates, ING says wider adoption of digital technologies, including AI-enabled tools, could ultimately lift labour productivity levels by 10-15% across Romania, Bulgaria, Croatia and Poland. The bank does not incorporate a large AI boost into its baseline scenario, but says faster adoption could add a few tenths of a percentage point to annual potential growth for several years if accompanied by investment in skills, management, R&D and financing.

Labour supply remains an issue

Demographics will continue to weigh on growth. Romania’s population declined from 23.2 million in 1990 to 19 million in 2025, while the population aged 20-64 fell from 13.4 million to 11 million. The OECD projects the latter figure could decline to 8.5 million by 2050.

However, ING notes that labour participation remains well below the EU average, leaving room to offset part of the demographic drag. In 2025, 69% of Romanians aged 20-64 were employed, compared with 76.1% across the EU. Higher participation among women, young people and older workers, alongside lower informality and more effective migration, could therefore support potential growth.

For ING, the central issue is increasingly how effectively Romania uses the assets it has built rather than how much more it can build. The bank sees potential growth of around 2.5% as sufficient for continued convergence with the EU, but at a slower pace than during the past two decades.
